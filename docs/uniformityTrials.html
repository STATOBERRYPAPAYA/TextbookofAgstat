17  Uniformity trials

A research worker would perform experiments under identical conditions to obtain valid results, but even with the most uniform land that can be selected, there will still be inherent variations in the soil. So, in order to maintain homogeneity, the experimenter should have a good idea of the nature and extent of fertility variation in the land. This can be obtained from the results of what are known as uniformity trials.

Uniformity trials can also be planned to determine the suitable size and shape of the plot and the number of plots in a block. Uniformity trials enable us to have an idea about the fertility variation of the field.

17.1 How a uniformity trial is performed

A uniformity trial is conducted to know the nature of the soil fertility gradient. In a uniformity trial, a particular variety of a crop is sown over the entire experimental field and uniformly managed throughout the growing season. Any fertilizer and other management inputs must be applied uniformly; fertilizer omission is not a defining requirement. At the time of harvest, a substantial border is removed from all sides of the field. The remainder of the field is divided into small plots, which are termed basic units. The size of the basic unit is decided by judgement, depending on the crop. Smaller basic units reveal finer spatial detail, but their size must also allow reliable harvesting and weighing at a practical cost. Very small units are not automatically more accurate. The produce from each basic unit is harvested and recorded separately. The yield differences between the basic units are taken as the measure of soil heterogeneity of the study area.

Several types of analysis are available to evaluate the pattern of soil heterogeneity based on uniformity trial data. We will discuss some of these procedures in detail.

17.2 Fertility contour map

An approach to describe the heterogeneity of land is to construct a fertility contour map. It is a simple but informative presentation of soil heterogeneity. It is constructed by taking the moving averages of the yields of unit plots and demarcating the regions of the same fertility by grouping together those areas which have yields of the same magnitude. Taking the moving average reduces the large random variation expected on small plots.

17.3 Serial correlation

Serial correlation describes association between neighbouring yields. Positive values indicate similarity between nearby units, but do not by themselves establish a directional fertility gradient. The sequence and treatment of boundaries must be stated. The following formula is for a circular sequence:

\[r_{s} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i}X_{i+1} - \dfrac{\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i} \right)^{2}}{n}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i}^{2} - \dfrac{\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i} \right)^{2}}{n}} \tag{17.1}\]

where \(X_{i}\) is the yield at position \(i\) in the chosen sequence, \(n\) is the number of units, and \(X_{n+1}=X_1\). In the worked example, successive columns or rows are traversed in alternating directions to form a serpentine sequence. The closing pair and turns between strips affect the coefficient, so it is a descriptive sequence statistic rather than a pure directional neighbour correlation.

17.4 Mean square between strips

This method is simpler to compute and has the same objective as the serial correlation. The units are first combined as horizontal and vertical strips. The variability between strips is measured in each direction by the mean square between strips. A mean square between vertical strips measures changes across columns, along the horizontal direction. A mean square between horizontal strips measures changes across rows, along the vertical direction. The strip orientation and the direction across which its totals vary must be distinguished; these are descriptive comparisons, not proof of a monotonic gradient.

\[\text{Sum of squares (Vertical)} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{c} V_{i}^{2}}{r} - \frac{G^{2}}{n} \tag{17.2}\]

\[\text{Sum of squares (Horizontal)} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r} H_{i}^{2}}{c} - \frac{G^{2}}{n} \tag{17.3}\]

\[\text{Mean square (Vertical)} = \frac{\text{Sum of squares (Vertical)}}{c - 1} \tag{17.4}\]

\[\text{Mean square (Horizontal)} = \frac{\text{Sum of squares (Horizontal)}}{r - 1} \tag{17.5}\]

where \(V_{i}\) and \(H_{i}\) are the sum totals of the basic units in the vertical and horizontal strips respectively; \(r\) is the number of rows and \(c\) is the number of columns. \(n\) is the total number of basic units, \(n = r \times c\). \(G\) is the total of all the basic units.

17.5 Fairfield Smith’s variance law

Smith (1938) gave an empirical relationship between variance and plot size (Smith 1938). He developed an empirical model representing the relationship between plot size and the variance of the mean per plot. This model is given by the equation

\[V_{x} = \frac{V_{1}}{x^{b}} \tag{17.6}\]

Taking logarithms on both sides,

\[\log V_{x} = \log V_{1} - b\log x \tag{17.7}\]

where \(x\) is the number of basic units in a plot, \(V_{x}\) is the variance of the mean per plot of \(x\) units, \(V_{1}\) is the variance of the mean per plot of one unit, and \(b\) is the negative of the slope in the log–log regression. The value of \(b\) is determined by the principle of least squares. \(b\) is called Smith’s index of soil heterogeneity. The index gives a single value as a quantitative measure of heterogeneity in the area.

17.5.1 Smith’s index of soil heterogeneity

Step 1. Combine the basic units to simulate plots of different sizes and shapes. Use only combinations that fit exactly into the whole area, i.e. the product of the number of simulated plots and the number of basic units per plot must equal the total number of basic units.

Step 2. For each of the simulated plots constructed in Step 1, compute the yield total \(T\) as the sum of the basic units that make up that plot, and compute the between-plot sum of squares \(Q_x\):

\[Q_x = \sum_{i=1}^{w}\frac{T_{i}^{2}}{x} - \frac{G^{2}}{rc} \tag{17.8}\]

where \(w = \dfrac{rc}{x}\) is the total number of simulated plots of size \(x\) basic units. \(r\) is the number of rows and \(c\) is the number of columns. \(G\) is the total of all the basic units.

Step 3. Compute the sample variance of the simulated plot means, which is the variance per basic unit area used here. Put \(N=rc\), \(z_i=T_i/x\) and \(\bar z=G/N\). Then

\[V_x=\frac{Q_x}{N-x}=\frac{Q_x}{rc-x} \tag{17.9}\]

To see why the divisor is \(N-x\), note that there are \(w=N/x\) plot means and

\[Q_x=x\sum_{i=1}^{w}(z_i-\bar z)^2.\]

Therefore,

\[V_x=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{w}(z_i-\bar z)^2}{w-1} =\frac{Q_x}{x(w-1)}.\]

This is equivalent to calculating the sample variance of the plot totals and dividing it by \(x^2\), as in Gomez and Gomez (1984, pp. 489–491). (Gomez and Gomez 1984) The sum of squares \(Q_x\) is an intermediate quantity, not a variance. At least two simulated plots are needed, so exclude \(x=N\) from variance calculations.

Some software uses a different field-scaled quantity, \(Q_x/(N-1)\). It agrees with \(V_x\) when \(x=1\) but differs for larger plots. Do not substitute that quantity into this calculation or combine its fitted index with the adjustment table below.

Step 4. Compare the variation for different shapes of the same size descriptively. The shapes are alternative partitions of the same observations, so their variance estimates are dependent. An ordinary two-sample F test or a chi-square variance test cannot automatically be applied to these values. Either retain the shapes separately, as in this example, or specify a descriptive averaging rule in advance. Formal inference requires a justified spatial model or independent replication.

For example, compare 2 × 1 m and 1 × 2 m plots of size 2 m² using the same definition of \(V_x\). A difference may be useful for choosing an orientation, but a non-significant test would not establish equality, even if a valid test were available.

Step 5. Using the values of the variance per unit area \(V_{x}\) computed in Steps 3 and 4, estimate the slope relating \(\log V_x-\log V_1\) to \(\log x\).

Using Fairfield Smith’s variance law, Equation 17.7 can be written as

\[\log V_{x} - \log V_{1} = -b\log x \tag{17.10}\]

Consider \(Y = \log V_{x} - \log V_{1}\). Then Equation 17.10 can be written in the form

\[Y = cX \quad \text{where} \quad c = -b, \ X = \log x\]

\(b\) is estimated by fitting a regression between \(Y\) and \(X\).

Step 6. Convert the fitted index \(b_{\text{cal}}\) to the table-adjusted index.

Why is an adjustment made? The fitted index describes the relationship between plot size and variance within the particular, finite area used for the uniformity trial. Smith introduced an adjustment to express this relationship for a theoretically unlimited field, so that the index is less tied to the size of the area observed. Here, an unlimited field is a mathematical reference for generalising the relationship; it does not mean that the trial must cover an unlimited area. (Smith 1938)

The adjustment therefore accounts for the finite area of the trial. Its size depends on the fitted index and on the area of a basic unit relative to the whole trial. It changes the index used in this generalisation; it does not change the observed yields or the variances already calculated. It is also separate from the degrees-of-freedom correction used to calculate a sample variance. The fitted index and the adjusted index should consequently be labelled separately when reporting the results.

Table 17.1 reproduces Table 12.5 of Gomez and Gomez (1984, p. 493); their interpolation procedure is on p. 494. (Gomez and Gomez 1984) The two adjusted-value columns refer to ranges of \(x_1/A\), where \(x_1\) is the physical area of one basic unit and \(A\) is the area of the whole trial, expressed in the same units. This dimensionless ratio equals \(1/N\) when all \(N\) basic units have equal area. It is not the size \(x\) of each simulated plot.

Use the table as follows:

  1. Calculate \(x_1/A\) and choose the adjusted-value column whose heading contains that ratio.
  2. In the first column, find the two computed-index values \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) that bracket \(b_{\text{cal}}\).
  3. In the selected adjusted-value column, read \(y_1\) opposite \(L_1\) and \(y_2\) opposite \(L_2\).
  4. Interpolate between those two entries:

\[b_{\text{adj}}=y_1+(b_{\text{cal}}-L_1) \frac{y_2-y_1}{L_2-L_1}. \tag{17.11}\]

The fraction \((b_{\text{cal}}-L_1)/(L_2-L_1)\) locates the fitted index between the two rows. Apply the same fraction to the change \(y_2-y_1\) in adjusted values. If the fitted index is exactly a tabulated value, read its adjusted value directly. The table gives an approximate adjustment within the stated ranges; do not extrapolate beyond its rows or columns without a justified method.

NoteNote

A relatively low Smith’s index is consistent with a slower reduction in variation as plot size increases, often associated with positive correlation between nearby units. Values between 0 and 1 are common under Smith’s empirical model; an unconstrained fitted slope need not give a value in this range. The index alone does not identify the spatial pattern.

17.6 Maximum curvature method

This method is used to find the optimal plot size for the experiment. In this method, the basic units of the uniformity trial are combined to form new units. The new units are formed by combining columns, rows, or both. The combination of columns and rows is done in such a way that no column or row is left out. For each set of units, the coefficient of variation (CV) is computed. A curve is plotted by taking the plot size (in terms of basic units) on the X-axis and the CV values on the Y-axis of a graph sheet. The point at which the curve takes a turn, i.e. an apparent bend, is located by inspection. Its horizontal coordinate provides a traditional graphical guide to plot size. The result depends on axis scales, the range of sizes examined and the objective; it is not a universal economic optimum.

17.7 Uniformity trial explained

Let us discuss in detail the procedures explained above using an example. Consider a rice crop field of 12 m × 17 m, uniformly managed throughout the growing season. Any fertilizer and other management inputs must be applied uniformly; fertilizer omission is not a defining requirement. At the time of harvest, a border of 1 m is removed from all sides of the field. The resultant effective area is now 10 m × 15 m. The entire field is divided into basic units of size 1 m × 1 m, and the yield (in grams) is noted from each basic unit. There are 150 basic units.

NoteNote

Here \(r = 15\), \(c = 10\), and the total number of basic units \(n = r \times c = 150\).

17.7.4 Smith’s index of soil heterogeneity

Example 17.1 Smith’s method describes how variance changes with plot size. Selecting a practical plot size also requires the available area, replication, precision and costs. Table 17.2 shows nine plot-size and shape arrangements created from the uniformity trial data in Figure 17.1.

Solution

It is recommended that the reader first read Section 17.5.1 and then read this example. Here, we illustrate how the variance per unit area for a plot of size 25 m² (denoted as \(V_{25}\)) is calculated. The width and length of the plot are 5 m × 5 m. The entire plot area in Figure 17.1 is divided into six plots of size 25 m² as shown in Figure 17.10.

Now consider the between-plot sum of squares in Equation 17.8, where \(w = \dfrac{rc}{x}\) is the total number of simulated plots of size \(x\) basic units. \(r\) is the number of rows and \(c\) is the number of columns. \(G\) is the total of all the basic units. The totals of each of the six plots are shown in Figure 17.11.

\[Q_{25} = \frac{21366^{2} + 21433^{2} + \ldots + 18811^{2}}{25} - \frac{(122777)^{2}}{150} = 218767.71\]

The variance per unit area is given by Equation 17.9,

\[V_{25}=\frac{Q_{25}}{150-25}=\frac{218767.71}{125}=1750.14.\]

There are \(150/25=6\) plot means. Their sample standard deviation is \(\sqrt{1750.141707}=41.8347\), and their mean is \(818.5133\). Hence \(CV=100(41.8347/818.5133)=5.11\%\), or 5.1% to one decimal place. The corresponding variances and CV values for all plot sizes and shapes are summarized in Table 17.3.

For size 5, the two orientations give the following between-plot sums of squares. Retain both orientations separately in the regression; do not treat them as independent samples for a variance test.

\(Q_x\) calculated for both plot shapes:

The descriptive ratio is \(456895.9/329859.5=1.39\). Thus the 5 × 1 orientation has about 39% more variation than the 1 × 5 orientation under the same scaling. Because both partitions use the same field observations, comparison with an ordinary \(F_{29,29}\) critical value is not justified. This ratio provides no valid significance test of orientation.

Now Smith’s index of soil heterogeneity is estimated as follows.

Fit a linear regression \(Y = cX\) and estimate the value of \(c\), where \(c = -b\).

Using the nine plot-size and shape arrangements separately and the sample variances defined in Step 3, the regression through the origin gives \(c=-0.360686601\), so \(b_{\text{cal}}=0.360686601\approx0.3607\). This is a descriptive fit: the alternative partitions reuse the same field observations. We retain both size-5 shapes rather than selecting one after a significance test.

If both the intercept and slope are estimated freely in \(\log V_x=a-b\log x\), the same nine arrangements give \(b=0.3468\). That is a different fit. The calculation above fixes the intercept at the observed \(\log V_1\), as specified by \(Y=cX\), and gives \(b=0.3607\). State which fitting method is used; the two estimates should not be substituted for one another without changing the model.

One basic unit has area \(x_1=1\text{ m}^2\) and the whole trial has area \(A=150\text{ m}^2\). Thus \(x_1/A=1/150=0.006667\), which selects the 0.001 to 0.01 column. The computed index 0.3607 lies between \(L_1=0.35\) and \(L_2=0.40\). In that column, the corresponding adjusted entries are \(y_1=0.403\) and \(y_2=0.443\).

\[b_{\text{adj}}=0.403+(0.3607-0.35)\frac{0.443-0.403}{0.40-0.35}=0.41156.\]

The fitted index is 21.4% of the way from 0.35 to 0.40, so the adjusted value is 21.4% of the way from 0.403 to 0.443. The result 0.41156 uses the rounded fitted index; retaining full precision gives 0.4115493, or 0.41155. Report which rounding convention is used. Thus, 0.3607 describes the fitted relationship within this trial, whereas approximately 0.41156 is the index after the finite-area adjustment prescribed by the table. The increase does not mean that the field has physically become more heterogeneous. Neither value is a percentage of soil heterogeneity. This adjusted index is a summary of variance reduction with increasing plot size; it does not establish a gradient direction or distinguish gradients from patches.

Important formulae

Serial correlation:

\[ r_s=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_iX_{i+1}-\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\right)^2}{n}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i^2-\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\right)^2}{n}} \]

Total number of basic units:

\[ n=r\times c \]

Vertical sum of squares:

\[ SS_V=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{c}V_i^2}{r}-\frac{G^2}{n} \]

Horizontal sum of squares:

\[ SS_H=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}H_i^2}{c}-\frac{G^2}{n} \]

Vertical mean square:

\[ MS_V=\frac{SS_V}{c-1} \]

Horizontal mean square:

\[ MS_H=\frac{SS_H}{r-1} \]

Fairfield Smith’s variance law:

\[ V_x=\frac{V_1}{x^b} \]

Logarithmic form of Smith’s variance law:

\[ \log V_x=\log V_1-b\log x \]

Difference form:

\[ \log V_x-\log V_1=-b\log x \]

Regression form:

\[ Y=cX \]

where

\[ Y=\log V_x-\log V_1 \]

and

\[ X=\log x \]

Relationship between regression coefficient and Smith’s index:

\[ c=-b \]

Number of simulated plots:

\[ w=\frac{rc}{x} \]

Between-plot sum of squares:

\[ Q_x=\sum_{i=1}^{w}\frac{T_i^2}{x}-\frac{G^2}{rc} \]

Variance per unit area:

\[ V_x=\frac{Q_x}{rc-x} \]

Coefficient of variation:

\[ CV=\frac{\text{Standard deviation}}{\text{Mean}}\times100 \]

Standard deviation from variance:

\[ SD=\sqrt{V_x} \]

Adjusted Smith’s index:

\[ \text{Adjusted }b=y_1+(b_{\text{cal}}-L_1)\frac{y_2-y_1}{L_2-L_1} \]

Descriptive ratio for two plot shapes (not an F test for dependent partitions):

\[ R=\frac{\text{Larger between-plot sum of squares}}{\text{Smaller between-plot sum of squares}} \]

CautionHistorical Insights

The wheat field that started it all

In 1910, at the Rothamsted Experimental Station in England, two researchers named W. B. Mercer and A. D. Hall carried out a classic uniformity trial. They took a single one-acre field of wheat, grew it under completely uniform treatment, and then harvested it as 500 tiny separate plots, each approximately one five-hundredth of an acre, recording the grain and straw yield of every plot on its own. When they looked at the results, something important became clear: even though every plot had received identical treatment, the yields varied considerably from one plot to the next. The variation revealed spatial differences despite uniform treatment; measurement error and other environmental influences can also contribute.

Their 1911 paper, The Experimental Error of Field Trials, showed that neighbouring plots tended to resemble one another, and that combining small plots into larger ones reduced the variability per unit area, exactly the ideas behind the fertility contour map, the serial correlation, and Fairfield Smith’s variance law that you have studied in this chapter. (Mercer and Hall 1911) The experiment provided an influential measure of field variability, and it convinced a generation of researchers that the size and shape of a plot, and the way plots are grouped into blocks, must be chosen deliberately rather than by guesswork. The patiently subdivided acre remains an influential illustration of the importance of field variability.
