23  Missing plot analysis

In agricultural field experiments the experimenter is often encountered with the situation that the observations of a particular treatment/ plot may be lost or may be affected by some external factors so that it would not be possible to analyse these observations by including it with the normal values. The observation on a treatment may get lost by various reasons like, attack of cattle or birds, manure may be dumped on the side, disease infestation and so on. Accidentally lost or unusable observations may require missing plot analysis. The reason for exclusion must be documented. A poor response caused by the treatment itself, such as treatment-induced plant death, must not automatically be discarded as a missing observation.

23.0.1 A missing observation in a CRD

In a CRD, an accidentally missing observation generally leaves unequal treatment sample sizes. Calculate each treatment mean from the observations available and fit the one-way model to those observations. If all \(v\) treatments remain represented, the residual degrees of freedom are \(n_{obs}-v\), where \(n_{obs}\) is the number actually observed. Use the unequal-replication standard error for comparisons. Filling the missing value with a mean and counting it as an observed replication would give a false impression of precision. The missingness assumptions still matter.

The analysis of RCBD and Latin-square data with missing plots may be done by different methods.

  1. the most commonly used method currently adopted is known as ‘method of analysis of non – orthogonal data’ which is highly computer based

  2. Method of substitution by Yates based on minimization of the error sum of squares and

  3. Analysis of the data with missing values by the technique of analysis of covariance, due to Bartlett.

The additive model must remain estimable from the observed plots. Missingness should not depend on unobserved responses in a way that biases the comparison; accidental sample loss differs from deliberately discarding low yields. With several missing plots, use the observed-data model and check its rank and residual degrees of freedom. Merely subtracting the number of missing plots from complete-data degrees of freedom is valid only when no additional effects become unestimable.

23.1 Covariance method (Bartlett’s approach)

Assume an imaginary covariate X taking values zero for every plot except the missing plot for which it will take the value 1 (or –1) . Now the value of the main variate Y = 0 for the missing plot, and the actual values for the remaining plots. The data will be analysed as per the ANCOVA technique of the respective design used.

With one missing value, the total and error degrees of freedom are each one less than in the complete design, provided the model remains estimable. In the indicator-covariate approach, the artificial zero is not a new observed response: the additional fitted indicator accounts for the missing position. Do not subtract a second degree of freedom after already using the reduced number of observed responses.

23.2 Method of substitution

  1. In this method we will calculate with the aid of a formula an estimate of the missing value. The formula will vary from design to design and actually it will not supply the exact missing value. But the procedure permits the researcher to complete the analysis without resorting to more complex procedures.

  2. Insert the estimated value in the missing position and work out the estimates of treatment means and error sum of squares.

  3. Some additional adjustments to treatment sum of squares are needed. In pair comparison also some changes are made.

  4. An iterative procedure is adopted when more than one observation is missing.

23.2.1 Randomized block design with a single missing value

  1. The missing value in RBD is estimated as

\[x=\frac{rB+vT-G}{(r-1)(v-1)} \tag{23.1}\]

where,
x = estimate of the missing data;
v = number of treatments;
r = number of replications;
B =Total of the observed values of the replication that contain the missing data
T = Total of the observed values of the treatment that contain the missing data
G = Grand total of all existing observations.

  1. Place the estimated value in its position and calculate the sums of squares using the RBD procedure. Subtract one degree of freedom from the total and error degrees of freedom. The completed-table error SS agrees with that from the additive model fitted to the observed data, but its treatment SS is inflated. Apply the adjustment below before testing treatments, rather than deciding whether to adjust after seeing the preliminary significance result. Direct fitting of the model to the observed responses is an alternative.

  2. Estimate of bias in the case of RBD:

\[Bias=\frac{(B+vT-G)^2}{v(v-1)(r-1)^2} \tag{23.2}\]

This bias is subtracted from the treatment sum of squares. Now test this actual treatment mean square against the mean square for error, and draw the conclusion about the significance of treatments.

  1. Pair comparison
  1. For comparing two treatment means in which one of them contains the missing observation:

\[CD=t_{\alpha/2,edf} \cdot \sqrt{MSE \cdot \left(\frac{2}{r} + \frac{v}{r(r-1)(v-1)}\right)} \tag{23.3}\]

  1. For comparing other pairs of treatment means (in which neither contains a missing observation):

\[CD=t_{\alpha/2,edf} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2\,MSE}{r}}\]

where \(t_{\alpha/2,edf}\) denotes the \(t\) table value at \((r-1)(v-1)-1\) degrees of freedom.

23.2.2 Latin square design with a single missing value

The same procedure as in RBD is used here also.

  1. The missing value in LSD is estimated as

\[x=\frac{v(R+C+T)-2G}{(v-1)(v-2)} \tag{23.4}\]

where
x = estimate of the missing data
v = number of treatments/ rows or blocks/ columns
R = Total of the observed values of the row that contain the missing data
C = Total of the observed values of the column that contain the missing data
T = Total of the observed values of the treatment that contain the missing data
G = Grand total of all existing observations.

  1. Carry out the analysis similar to the above method after substitution. The estimate of bias in this case is

\[Bias=\frac{[(v-1)T+R+C-G]^2}{[(v-1)(v-2)]^2} \tag{23.5}\]

  1. For pairwise comparison involving a treatment with a missing observation (comparing two treatment means in which one of them contains a missing observation):

\[CD=t_{\alpha/2,edf} \cdot \sqrt{MSE \cdot \left(\frac{2}{v} + \frac{1}{(v-1)(v-2)}\right)} \tag{23.6}\]

23.3 Chapter Summary

Important formulae

For a CRD, analyse the observed responses using their actual treatment sample sizes. If all \(v\) treatments remain represented:

\[edf=n_{obs}-v,\qquad SE(\bar Y_i-\bar Y_j)=\sqrt{MSE\left(\frac{1}{n_i}+\frac{1}{n_j}\right)}\]

For one missing observation in an RCBD with \(v\) treatments and \(r\) blocks:

\[\hat x=\frac{rB+vT-G}{(r-1)(v-1)}, \qquad edf=(r-1)(v-1)-1\]

Here \(B\) and \(T\) are the observed totals of the affected block and treatment, and \(G\) is the observed grand total.

For one missing observation in a Latin square of order \(v\):

\[\hat x=\frac{v(R+C+T)-2G}{(v-1)(v-2)}, \qquad edf=(v-1)(v-2)-1\]

Here \(R\), \(C\) and \(T\) are the observed totals of the affected row, column and treatment. In both substitution formulae, all totals exclude the missing value. Use the bias corrections and comparison formulae in the chapter; do not treat the substituted value as an independent observation.

Quick revision

  • Record why an observation is missing or unusable.
  • A poor response caused by a treatment must not automatically be discarded.
  • A substituted value is an estimate, not a recovered observation.
  • The CRD can usually be fitted directly with unequal treatment sample sizes.
  • A missing plot destroys the complete balance of an RCBD or Latin square.
  • With one missing plot, the usual complete-design error degrees of freedom decrease by one, provided the model remains estimable.
  • Substitution minimizes error SS; treatment SS and comparison standard errors require the stated adjustments.
  • Fitting the model to observed responses is an alternative to substitution.
  • Missingness related to an unobserved response can bias the analysis; a calculation alone does not resolve this problem.

CautionHistorical Insights

Frank Yates and the art of the missing value

In the field, experiments rarely go exactly to plan. A goat breaks into a plot, a flood washes out a corner, a technician mislabels a sample, and suddenly one of the neatly balanced yields is simply gone. In the early days of agricultural statistics this was a serious problem, because Fisher’s elegant analysis of variance relied on the tidy balance of equal numbers in every treatment and block. A single hole in the data destroyed that balance and, with it, the simple arithmetic of the analysis.

The solution came from Frank Yates, Fisher’s brilliant younger colleague at the Rothamsted Experimental Station. In the early 1930s Yates worked out that a missing observation could be replaced by an estimated value, chosen precisely so as to minimize the error sum of squares, allowing the standard analysis to proceed almost unchanged. His formulae, one for the randomized block design, another for the Latin square, let researchers recover nearly all the information from a damaged experiment with only a small, correctable bias in the treatment sum of squares and the loss of a single degree of freedom. Yates set this out in his 1933 paper on the analysis of replicated experiments with missing values, and the “missing plot technique” quickly became a standard part of every field experimenter’s toolkit. It is a fine example of the statistician’s craft: not discarding hard-won data because of an accident, but finding a principled way to make the most of what remains. (Yates 1933)
